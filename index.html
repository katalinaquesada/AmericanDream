01 / THE ARGUMENT

The American Dream looks forward.

But looking forward does not guarantee that we are actually moving forward.

In The Great Gatsby, Jay Gatsby believes wealth can reconstruct the past and purchase the future he wants. In Fahrenheit 451, Guy Montag begins by accepting a society that has traded knowledge and reflection for entertainment and comfort.

Fitzgerald and Bradbury expose different failures of the same American promise: freedom without equality, success without happiness, and progress without self-knowledge.

Gatsby reaches toward a green light he can never truly possess. Montag walks through fire and discovers that destruction can also become the beginning of change.

FITZGERALD / THE GREAT GATSBY

The green light

Gatsby transforms the future into an object: something distant, beautiful, and almost reachable.

Symbol: aspiration. It becomes dangerous when the dream depends on wealth, status, and rewriting reality.

02 / THE GREAT GATSBY

F. SCOTT FITZGERALD

A dream with a price.

✦

A story of reinvention, longing, and the limits of wealth.

CHAPTERS 1, 5 & 9

Gatsby appears to embody the American Dream: he becomes wealthy, builds a spectacular house, throws extravagant parties, and creates a new identity.

Yet Fitzgerald makes the dream unstable. Gatsby’s wealth does not erase the social boundaries separating him from Daisy’s world.

“Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us.”

— F. Scott Fitzgerald, Chapter 9

The green light is powerful because Gatsby does not simply want Daisy. He wants the future Daisy represents: status, belonging, permanence, and proof that his transformation has succeeded.

His tragedy is larger than romantic failure. The dream itself is structured around distance. Gatsby can accumulate money, but he cannot purchase the history or social identity that would make him belong completely to the world he desires.

03 / FAHRENHEIT 451

RAY BRADBURY

A future without questions.

A story of conformity, curiosity, and awakening.

PARTS I, II & III

Bradbury imagines a future where books are dangerous because they encourage people to slow down, think deeply, and encounter uncomfortable ideas.

Montag begins as a firefighter whose job is to burn books rather than save buildings.

“It was a pleasure to burn.”

— Ray Bradbury, Part I

The statement becomes ironic as Montag changes. What once gave him pleasure becomes the symbol of a society that has confused comfort with happiness.

Unlike Gatsby, Montag’s transformation requires him to stop chasing the future and start questioning the future he has been given.

Bradbury complicates the American Dream: freedom is meaningless if people voluntarily surrender curiosity, memory, individuality, and the ability to disagree.

05 / THE STATEMENT

What kind of future should we choose?

Gatsby’s story asks who is allowed to belong. Montag’s asks what happens when people stop thinking for themselves. Together, the novels challenge us to measure progress by more than wealth, comfort, or appearances.

A future worth reaching for requires equality, curiosity, and the courage to question.
